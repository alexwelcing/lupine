⚑ Coach's note — why not the $150M post from the model?

The value-model page prices the company at a $150M post. That is the right number to show — it's the Series-A trajectory the 30-year arc supports. It is the wrong number to raise the seed on: a $3M lead into a $150M post owns 2%, and no seed lead accepts 2%. Leads price to own 10–20%.

To make a $3M+ lead easy, the seed post lands around $25–32M — there a $3M check buys ~10–12%, exactly the ownership a deep-tech lead needs to justify the work. Keep the $150M in the room as the ceiling; price the entry where leads can actually win. Show the gap on purpose: "we're pricing the seed conservatively; the model shows where this goes."

★ The single biggest "easy" lever — run two tracks in parallel

Track A (equity): a time-boxed, competitive process — all first meetings in the same 2 weeks so momentum and FOMO compound, not a serial drip that lets each fund wait on the others.

Track B (non-dilutive): NSF/DOE SBIR, ARPA-E, DARPA, AFWERX — this directly delivers your "federal contract in flight" milestone, de-risks the company for every equity investor, and means you're raising from a position of "we don't strictly need it" — the position that closes rounds.

⚑ Solo-founder reality — plan for it deliberately

You're raising solo. Not disqualifying — solo technical founders who have shipped and published do get funded — but it sharpens three moves. (1) Target solo-friendly leads. Some funds reflexively pass on solo founders; don't burn cycles there. Lux, DCVC, 1517, and Founders Fund are more open to it than most. (2) Make the early team the explicit use of funds — name the first 3–4 hires by role, and stack 2–3 advisors now (cheap, fast de-risking). (3) If an additive second founder is reachable, that's the highest-leverage move available — it lifts the valuation and removes the single most common reason a seed partner passes. Weigh a 4–8 week pause to close one.

04 / The process

Six to eight weeks, run in parallel.

A raise feels hard when it's serial and open-ended. It gets easy when it's batched and time-boxed: create a real deadline, stack first meetings into one window, convert a lead, then close the fill on the lead's momentum.

Week 0Prep

Lock the materials & the list

Deck, one-pager, the value-model site, data room, and this target list finalized. Map every warm path. Start non-dilutive (SBIR/grant) applications now — they run on their own clock.

Week 1–2Open

Fire all warm intros at once

Every intro request goes out the same week. Batch first meetings into a tight window so funds sense parallel motion. Aim for ~15 first conversations. Soft-circle interest in the pipeline above.

Week 3–4Convert the lead

Drive the most-engaged fund to a partner meeting → term sheet

Give your hottest lead candidate a slight head start and a reason to move ("we're seeing strong interest; I'd love you to anchor"). A lead + price unlocks everyone else. This is the gate the whole round hinges on.

Week 5–6Fill

Close the remaining ~$5M on the lead's terms

Go back to warm participants and strategics: "round's led by [lead] at [terms], closing [date], room for a few more." SAFEs at the same cap sign fast. Allocation pressure does the selling.

Week 7–8Sign

Papers, wire, done

Counsel runs docs in parallel with the fill. Collect signatures, confirm wires, file. Send a warm "we're funded, here's what's next" note to everyone who helped — they're your Series-A network.

06 / Objection bank

The hard questions — and the crisp answers.

Pre-load these. The goal isn't to win the argument; it's to show you've already thought past the objection. Practice each until it's a reflex.

Is this a company or a research project?
Answer A company that started with truth. The peer-reviewed paper is the wedge, not the product — it's how we earn the right to be the canonical audit layer. Revenue comes from cloud-hosted validation, federal contracts, and enterprise support; the open-core engine is the adoption flywheel. The research is the moat's foundation, the way a published benchmark made Hugging Face the default.
You're a solo founder — that's a real concern at $8M.
Answer — own it, don't flinch Solo today, and the one person who proved this thesis. Alex authored the methodology (working paper, machine-checked formal core) and shipped the Rust audit engine alone, before raising — domain authority plus demonstrated execution, the rare combination VCs actually back in a solo founder. The primary purpose of this round is the early team — senior MLIP/DFT engineering, scientific software, the first commercial hire — built deliberately around a de-risked thesis, not bolted on for optics. Surround the round with 2–3 named advisors from the materials-ML world; they de-risk you cheaply. If a genuinely additive technical or commercial second founder is reachable, closing them before/during the raise is the single biggest valuation unlock available — worth pausing 4–8 weeks for.
You'll build DFT + MD + ML in 24 months? VASP took 30 years.
Answer We're not rebuilding VASP. We're the validation layer on top of the engines that already exist (MACE, NequIP, LAMMPS, the foundation MLIPs). The 24-month plan ships the audit substrate + an open benchmark + a DFT-engine alpha — not a production replacement for a 30-year codebase. The unified engine is the 10-year arc; the seat we're claiming now is trust, and that's a software-and-methodology problem, not a 30-year physics problem.
$150M valuation for a seed?
Answer That's the model's ceiling, not the ask. We're pricing the seed at ~$30M post — deliberately conservative — and showing you the $150M because it's where the 30-year arc points. We'd rather you underwrite the floor (a $332M-intrinsic software-of-record DCF that already clears the entry) and treat the ceiling as the option you're getting for free.
How do you make money before "programmable matter" in 2045?
Answer Phase 1 is a real business today: validation/UQ tooling and benchmarks for the teams already deploying foundation MLIPs, cloud-hosted simulation, federal contracts (the sovereignty buyers need open-core), and enterprise support. The floor math is built entirely on classical-materials acceleration — zero credit for the moonshot. The near-term revenue doesn't depend on the far-term vision.
Why won't Microsoft or Google just build this in-house?
Answer Two reasons. (1) Open-core is the architecture, not a license choice — sovereign and defense buyers can't adopt a closed substrate from a hyperscaler, so an in-house clone can't serve the buyer base we can. (2) The moat is canonical-ness: the audit layer is only trusted if it's the neutral place the field agrees on truth. A Microsoft-owned validator isn't neutral. That's also exactly why they'd rather acquire us — see the strategic-acquirer section of the model.
Open source — how do you capture value if you give it away?
Answer Same way GitHub, Hugging Face, Datadog, and Databricks did. The open engine drives adoption; revenue comes from the hosted platform, the benchmark/leaderboard position, enterprise/compliance, and support. Open-core isn't charity — it's how you become the default, and the default captures 0.1–1.4% of an enormous ecosystem (that's the comps table). Closed loses the sovereign buyers and never becomes canonical.
Why now, and why you?
Answer Now: foundation MLIPs just crossed the accuracy bar (2024), so the bottleneck moved from "can we simulate?" to "can we trust it?" — the exact gap we fill, and no one owns it yet. You: we shipped the methodology (peer-reviewed) and the engine before raising; we measured the physics first. The team that publishes the canonical benchmark owns the category, and we're already doing it.
