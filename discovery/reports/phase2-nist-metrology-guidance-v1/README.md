# NIST guidance for the metrology risk contract

NIST TN1297 supports the contract's main distinction: measurement uncertainty requires an explicit interpretation and coverage model before it can serve as a probabilistic premise. The official guidance includes covariance in combined uncertainty and qualifies the usual `k=2`, approximately 95% interpretation. It does not validate BAM, Lupine, a predictive model or a recommendation pool.

Section 5.1 treats combined standard uncertainty as the estimated standard deviation of a measurement result. It combines component standard uncertainties, including appropriate covariances, from Type A and Type B evaluations. Calling this propagation an RSS method does not justify dropping covariance or assuming independence. An alternative union-bound argument can avoid a covariance premise, but still requires valid marginal event bounds; that is the separate mathematical contract, not a new theorem supplied by NIST.

Section 5.2 addresses correction for recognized significant systematic effects and the uncertainty of the correction. Its first note distinguishes uncertainty due to incomplete knowledge of a correction from the systematic error itself. Its second note acknowledges that applying corrections may sometimes be impractical. These statements do not determine whether any particular BAM correction is significant or adequately accounted for. A calibration deviation or a traceability flag cannot simply be used as an uncertainty budget.

Sections 5.4 and 6.2 make the coverage conditions explicit. The approximately 68% interpretation of one combined-standard-uncertainty interval, and approximately 95% interpretation with `k=2`, require the stated distributional and uncertainty-estimation qualifications: normality or its applicable approximation, and negligible uncertainty in the combined standard uncertainty. Section 5.4 discusses comparable contributions and an adequate linear approximation among circumstances supporting approximate normality. A scale factor alone supplies neither a universal tail law nor a deterministic enclosure or exact nonlinear certificate.

Section 5.5 distinguishes the statistical term “confidence interval” from the guidance's broader uncertainty-interval usage. It names necessary conditions, including Type A evaluation of all contributing uncertainty components; it does not state that Type A classification alone is sufficient. The guidance describes coverage as a fraction of the distribution characterized by the measurement result and its uncertainty. Section 6.1 applies the same qualification to expanded uncertainty. The risk contract must therefore state the probability interpretation and source of randomness instead of silently converting publisher terminology into a new frequentist, posterior or selection-conditional assertion.

Section 6.1 defines expanded uncertainty as `U = k uc`. Sections 6.3–6.4 explain why determining a precise coverage level from `k` is difficult: it requires detailed information about the contributing distributions and their combination, and practical procedures may be approximate. The example following §6.5 discusses a t-factor when the combined standard uncertainty has low degrees of freedom. No distribution, factor, degree of freedom or replacement interval was chosen in this work.
