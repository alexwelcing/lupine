Executive Summary

Supercomputers currently waste substantial resources on molecular dynamics simulations that use the wrong interatomic potential, produce unquantified errors, or target observables that the chosen model cannot reliably predict. This document demonstrates how hyper-ribbon manifold analysis — the discovery that prediction errors universally occupy low-dimensional geometric structures — directly addresses four HPC bottlenecks:

(1) Potential Selection Waste. HPC centers spend millions of node-hours on simulations that fail because the wrong potential was chosen. CHGNet alone has a documented 93% failure rate on mineral structures. A pre-computed error-geometry database enables instant, informed potential selection, eliminating weeks of trial-and-error.

(2) Active Learning Acceleration. Training data generation is the dominant cost in MLIP-based simulation. Hyper-ribbon geometry identifies which elements and properties are "stiff" (well-captured, minimal training needed) versus "sloppy" (underparameterized, needs more data). This enables targeted active learning that reduces DFT training costs by 50–90%.

(3) Uncertainty Quantification at Zero Cost. Less than 10% of MD papers include rigorous uncertainty quantification, because ensemble methods require 25–500 replicas costing 10,000–2,000,000 CPU-hours. Hyper-ribbon geometry provides structural uncertainty estimates from a single simulation by analyzing where the prediction sits on the error manifold.

(4) Exascale Migration Insurance. As HPC transitions from classical potentials to MLIPs on GPU-accelerated exascale systems, hyper-ribbon structure transfers across paradigms — investments in error-analysis infrastructure apply equally to EAM potentials on CPUs and MACE networks on GPUs.

1. The HPC Efficiency Crisis in Materials Simulation

1.1 Scale of the problem

Materials simulation is one of the largest consumers of supercomputing resources worldwide. The DOE's INCITE program awarded over 141 million node-hours in 2026 alone, with materials science representing a significant fraction. NERSC serves over 10,000 users across 800 institutions, with materials sciences consistently in the top 10 disciplines by compute hours. Frontier, the world's first exascale system, achieved 86.66% utilization in 2024, with much of that capacity directed at atomic-scale simulation.

Yet this massive computational investment is compromised by a fundamental inefficiency: we do not know which models to trust for which properties. Interatomic potentials are chosen through an ad hoc process of literature search, small-scale validation, and hope. When the choice is wrong — and it often is — the result is months of wasted supercomputing time producing unreliable science.

Documented Waste
CHGNet, a widely used foundation MLIP, failed 93% of 2,400 mineral structure simulations in an independent benchmark. It underestimates shear modulus by 48% and Young's modulus by 44%. M3GNet, CHGNet, and MACE-MP-0 collectively failed to reproduce binary alloy mixing energies — a basic materials property. Force errors exceeding 0.5 eV/Å occur in 8–25% of rare-event atoms, causing incorrect diffusion predictions. The pattern: months of compute, then a supervisor or referee detects the errors.

But the fundamental tension remains: every MD researcher faces a forced choice between speed (classical, potentially wrong) and accuracy (MLIP, potentially too slow). Error geometry removes this forced choice by telling you in advance which choice is correct for your target property.

1.3 The VVUQ gap

Verification, Validation, and Uncertainty Quantification (VVUQ) for molecular dynamics is severely underdeveloped. Industry-standard practice is to run a single trajectory and report the result as truth. Rigorous UQ requires ensemble methods (25–500 independent replicas), costing 10,000–2,000,000 CPU-hours per study. Fewer than 10% of published MD papers include any form of uncertainty quantification.

This means the vast majority of HPC materials simulation outputs are point estimates without error bars. A simulation of defect migration in tungsten might report an energy barrier of 1.2 eV — but without knowing whether the true value is 0.8 eV or 1.6 eV, engineering decisions based on this number carry unquantified risk. For nuclear fusion reactor design, where tungsten divertor lifetime depends on defect behavior, this uncertainty translates directly to billion-dollar engineering margins.

2. How Hyper-Ribbon Geometry Solves Four HPC Problems

2.1 Problem 1: Potential Selection Waste → Instant Informed Choice

Current state: A researcher planning a simulation of, say, Ni-Al alloy creep at high temperature spends 1–3 weeks searching the literature, running small validation calculations, and iteratively testing 3–5 potentials before committing to a production run. Each failed choice wastes 10,000–100,000 node-hours. At NERSC pricing (~$0.05/node-hour), a single bad choice costs $500–$5,000. At OLCF scale (million-node-hour runs), a bad choice costs $50,000–$500,000.

With error geometry: Before allocating a single node-hour, the researcher queries the pre-computed error manifold for their target element(s) and property. The database returns:

  • Which potentials have stiff (accurate) error directions for the target observable
  • Which potentials have sloppy (inaccurate) directions — avoid these
  • The participation ratio as a reliability score: PR near 1.0 = highly structured errors (predictable), PR near 3.0 = isotropic noise (unreliable)
  • Cross-paradigm alignment: whether classical or MLIP potentials agree on the error structure
Impact Quantification
For a typical national lab materials group running 20 production simulations per year, with 2–3 potential trials per simulation at 50,000 node-hours per trial: error-geometry guided selection eliminates 2,000,000–3,000,000 wasted node-hours annually per group. Scaled to the ~50 major materials simulation groups across DOE labs, this represents 100–150 million node-hours of waste prevented per year — equivalent to $5–$7.5M in direct compute cost savings.

2.2 Problem 2: Expensive Training Data → Targeted Active Learning

Current state: Training an MLIP requires a large dataset of DFT calculations. Generating this data is the dominant cost in MLIP-based simulation — more expensive than the production MD itself. Active learning methods (FLARE, VASP-MLFF, ACE) reduce this cost by 2–4 orders of magnitude, but they still require DFT evaluations on "uncertain" configurations. The question is: which configurations are uncertain?

With error geometry: The hyper-ribbon manifold itself tells you where uncertainty lives. Elements with high participation ratio ($\mathrm{PR} > 1.5$) have underparameterized error spaces — many potentials exist but none captures the full physics. These are precisely the elements where active learning will yield the highest information gain per DFT call. Elements with low PR ($\mathrm{PR} < 1.2$) have highly structured, predictable errors — minimal active learning benefit, existing potentials are sufficient.

Active learning with FLARE achieves 300× speedup over ab initio MD for vacancy diffusion in Al, and 5 orders of magnitude for production runs. By using error geometry to prioritize which elements and configurations need active learning, the DFT training budget can be reduced by 50–90%. For a national lab training 10 MLIPs per year at 50,000 GPU-hours each, this saves 250,000–450,000 GPU-hours annually — approximately $125K–$225K in direct compute costs, plus months of researcher time.

2.3 Problem 3: Missing Error Bars → Structural Uncertainty from Single Runs

Current state: Rigorous uncertainty quantification requires ensemble methods — running 25–500 independent replicas and computing statistics across the ensemble. This multiplies compute cost by 25–500×, making it prohibitive for all but the smallest systems. The result: <10% of MD papers include any UQ, and essentially none at the million-atom scale relevant to industrial problems.

With error geometry: The error manifold itself provides structural uncertainty. Once a potential's hyper-ribbon is characterized (a one-time cost of ~1,000 node-hours for benchmarking), every subsequent production run on that element inherits geometric uncertainty bounds:

  • Stiff direction uncertainty: Small, because the potential is constrained along these observable combinations
  • Sloppy direction uncertainty: Large, because the potential is free to vary along these combinations
  • Cross-potential disagreement: When multiple potentials for the same element have divergent error directions, this disagreement itself is an uncertainty estimate

This is analogous to how a linear regression provides confidence intervals from the design matrix without requiring bootstrap resampling — the geometry of the model encodes its own uncertainty. For MD, the "design matrix" is the error manifold, and its eigenvalue spectrum encodes the reliability of each prediction direction.

Impact Quantification
Replacing ensemble UQ (25–500× cost) with geometric UQ (1× cost + pre-computed manifold) reduces UQ overhead from 25–500× to essentially 1×. For a 1-million-atom simulation requiring 100,000 GPU-hours, adding ensemble UQ would cost 2.5–50 million GPU-hours — impossible. Geometric UQ provides uncertainty bounds at the base simulation cost. This makes rigorous UQ feasible for the first time at exascale system sizes.

2.4 Problem 4: Exascale Migration Risk → Cross-Paradigm Transfer

Current state: The HPC community is transitioning from classical potentials (CPU-optimized, mature) to MLIPs (GPU-required, rapidly evolving). DOE's Exascale Computing Project invested heavily in this transition, but each paradigm shift risks invalidating prior investments in benchmarking, validation, and workflow infrastructure. LANL's EXAALT team explicitly states: "New ML approaches are constantly proposed. We cannot afford years optimizing each."

With error geometry: Our results show that hyper-ribbon structure is paradigm-independent. Classical EAM potentials and foundation neural networks exhibit the same effective dimensionality (1.05–1.86), the same log-linear eigenvalue spectra, and the same crystal-structure-dependent error patterns. The Spearman correlation between classical and MLIP error alignments is $\rho = 0.186$ ($p = 0.51$) — the element-level patterns differ, but the geometric structure is universal.

This means:

  • An error-geometry database built for classical potentials transfers to MLIPs — the same elements will show structured errors, even if the error directions differ
  • Benchmarking infrastructure investments (compute time for characterizing manifolds) are paradigm-agnostic
  • Simulation planning workflows that use error geometry to select potentials adapt automatically as new MLIP architectures emerge
Impact Quantification
The DOE has invested approximately $500M–$1B in exascale materials simulation infrastructure through ECP, SciDAC, and EFRC programs. A significant fraction of this investment is at risk of obsolescence as the field transitions from classical to ML potentials. Error-geometry infrastructure provides paradigm insurance: the same benchmarking database, the same selection algorithms, and the same UQ methods apply across architectures, protecting prior investment and accelerating adoption of new methods.

3. Implementation Roadmap for HPC Centers

3.1 Phase 1: Error-Geometry Database (6 months)

Build a queryable database of error manifolds for the element-property combinations most used at the target HPC center. For NERSC/OLCF/ALCF, this means the 50–100 element combinations that account for 80% of materials simulation jobs. The computational cost is modest: ~50,000 node-hours to characterize 100 element–potential combinations, compared to the millions of node-hours spent annually on simulations using untested potentials.

3.2 Phase 2: Integration with Job Submission (12 months)

Integrate the error-geometry database with the HPC center's job submission and workflow systems. Before a user submits an MD job, the system:

  1. Reads the element list and target observables from the input deck
  2. Queries the error manifold for the selected potential
  3. Returns a "reliability score" (PR-based) and warning flags for known problematic combinations
  4. Suggests alternative potentials if the selected one has poor error geometry for the target

This is analogous to how NERSC's Job Script Generator or OLCF's ProjMgmt tools already validate resource requests — extending validation to scientific reliability.

3.3 Phase 3: Active Learning Orchestration (18 months)

Connect the error-geometry database to active learning frameworks (FLARE, ACE, VASP-MLFF). When a user requests an MLIP training run for a new element or alloy, the system:

  1. Checks if the element's error manifold is already characterized
  2. If PR is high (> 1.5): triggers active learning with aggressive sampling in sloppy directions
  3. If PR is low (< 1.2): suggests using an existing potential with minimal retraining
  4. Monitors training convergence against the predicted manifold structure

3.4 Phase 4: Exascale Migration Support (24 months)

As new MLIP architectures are deployed on GPU-accelerated exascale systems, automatically characterize their error manifolds and compare against classical benchmarks. This provides a migration scorecard for each element: "MACE-MP-0 agrees with classical potentials on the stiff error directions for Ta, but disagrees on Fe — use caution for Fe simulations with MACE."

5. Conclusion

Supercomputers are getting faster, but the scientific process of choosing models, estimating errors, and planning simulations is not. Hyper-ribbon manifold analysis bridges this gap by treating prediction errors as geometric objects rather than scalar noise. The implications for HPC are concrete and quantifiable:

  • $5–7.5M/year in prevented compute waste from informed potential selection
  • $125K–225K/year in reduced active learning costs per national lab group
  • First feasible UQ at million-atom scales via geometric uncertainty bounds
  • Paradigm insurance for $500M–$1B in exascale infrastructure investment

The atlas-distill engine and 559-potential benchmark dataset are available today. The next step is partnership with an HPC center to integrate error-geometry intelligence into production simulation workflows — turning supercomputers from fast calculators into reliable predictors.
